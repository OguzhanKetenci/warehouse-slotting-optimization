# Methodology

## 1. Data cleaning
- Removed cancelled invoices (invoice numbers starting with `C`).
- Removed rows with non-positive quantity or price.
- Removed non-product codes (e.g. `POST`, `DOT`, `M`, `BANK CHARGES`); valid product codes are 5 digits with an optional letter suffix.

## 2. Warehouse interpretation of the data
- **Order (invoice)** = one picking task.
- **Order line** = one visit to a storage location.
- **Pick frequency** of a SKU = number of order lines containing it.

## 3. ABC classification
- Cumulative share thresholds: A ≤ 80%, B ≤ 95%, C = rest.
- Computed twice (by pick frequency and by revenue) to show that the two classifications differ.

## 4. Synthetic warehouse layout (Module 2)
- Single block with parallel aisles, one front and one back cross aisle. 40 aisles, each with a left and a right rack of 50 slots (1 m per slot): 4,000 slots for 3,791 SKUs, 209 spare.
- Aisle length 50 m; aisle centre lines 3 m apart. Aisle *j* (0-based) is at x = 1.5 + 3*j* m; slot *k* (0-based) is at y = *k* + 0.5 m from the front cross aisle.
- The depot (pick-up and drop-off point) is at the left end of the front cross aisle, x = 0, y = 0.
- **Slot walking distance** = x + y (rectilinear, via the front cross aisle and the aisle). Slots are ranked by this distance; ties are broken by aisle, position, side. Cross-aisle width and the lateral movement between the two racks of an aisle are ignored.

## 5. S-shape routing
- Input per order: the set of distinct SKUs (one visit per SKU, even if the invoice lists it on several lines) mapped to slots.
- Every aisle that contains a pick is traversed end to end. The picker moves between aisles along the front and back cross aisles alternately.
- If the number of aisles with picks is **even**, the route ends at the front cross aisle and returns to the depot. If it is **odd**, the last (rightmost) aisle is entered from the front and left again from its farthest pick.
- Closed form used in the code: with *n* aisles containing picks, *x*<sub>max</sub> the x of the rightmost of them and *y*<sub>far</sub> the farthest pick in that aisle,
  `distance = 2 * x_max + L * (n - n mod 2) + 2 * y_far * (n mod 2)`, where *L* is the aisle length. The horizontal part is always 2 * x_max because the picker goes out to the rightmost aisle and back.
- The formula is verified in `tests/test_routing.py` against three hand-calculated routes (1, 2 and 3 aisles: 42.0 m, 115.0 m, 176.0 m) and against an independent waypoint-by-waypoint simulation on random orders.

## 6. Slotting scenarios
- **Random (baseline):** SKUs are placed in a random subset of the slots. 10 seeds (0-9); results are reported as mean and min-max.
- **Class-based ABC:** slots, sorted by walking distance, are split into three zones sized to the number of A, B and C SKUs (ABC by pick frequency from Module 1). A SKUs go to the nearest zone, then B, then C. Placement inside a zone is random (10 seeds). Spare slots stay empty at the far end. This is the most common practical policy.
- **Full velocity:** SKUs are sorted by pick frequency (ties by SKU code); the fastest SKU takes the nearest slot, the next fastest the next nearest, and so on. Deterministic.
- SKU ranking uses the order-line count from Module 1 (`pick_lines`); routing uses distinct (order, SKU) visits (517,254 visits from 527,725 lines).

## 7. Evaluation and KPIs (Modules 2 and 3)
- Every order of the last 12 months (19,773) is routed in every scenario; no sampling.
- **Avg. distance per order** = mean S-shape route length over all orders (m). **Total annual distance** = sum over all orders (km). For random and class-based scenarios both are means over the seeds.
- **Change vs. random baseline** = total distance of the scenario / mean total distance of the random seeds - 1.
- **Picks from nearest 20% of slots** = share of (order, SKU) visits served from the 800 slots closest to the depot by walking distance.
- **Estimated annual walking time** = total distance / 1.0 m/s. Walking only; picking, handling and congestion are excluded.
- `reports/kpi_summary.xlsx` holds the KPI table, all assumptions and the slot assignment of the 100 fastest SKUs. Its numbers are read from `data/processed/scenario_results.csv` and checked against it after saving.

## 8. Out-of-sample check (Module 4)
- **Purpose:** Modules 2-3 rank SKUs with the same period they are evaluated on. Module 4 measures how much of the saving remains when SKUs are ranked on past orders only, on two independent splits, and decomposes the gap into a new-SKU effect and a frequency-drift effect.

### 8.1 Splits
- **6+6 months** (within the Module 1 year): each invoice is assigned by its timestamp. Orders before 1 June 2011 are the *history* (8,067 orders, Dec 2010 - May 2011); orders on or after are the *future/evaluation* set (11,706 orders, Jun - Dec 2011). SKU universe = all 3,791 SKUs of the full year (Module 1's `sku_velocity.csv`), unchanged from the first version of this module. The two halves are unequal in order count and the evaluation half includes the autumn peak.
- **Year-over-year**: *history* = the raw sheet `Year 2009-2010`, cleaned with Module 1's rules and trimmed to invoices starting in [1 Dec 2009, 1 Dec 2010). *Future* = Module 1's `clean_lines.csv` (sheet `Year 2010-2011`), trimmed to invoices starting in [1 Dec 2010, 1 Dec 2011). Two full, non-overlapping 12-month windows. SKU universe = only SKUs picked in the future window (3,789 SKUs) - a warehouse does not reserve a slot for an item it has never stocked. If that count exceeded the default 4,000-slot layout (40 aisles), Module 4 would add aisles (`ceil(n_skus / (2 x 50))`) and log it; at 3,789 SKUs this was not needed, so both splits use the same 40-aisle layout.
- **Cross-sheet overlap:** the two source sheets share an exact 9-day window (1-9 Dec 2010) that is byte-for-byte identical in both (verified directly on the raw file). Before windowing, every history row is matched against the future rows on all business columns (Invoice, StockCode, Description, Quantity, InvoiceDate, Price, CustomerID, Country), matching the *k*-th occurrence of a repeated row to the *k*-th occurrence on the other side (so a value that legitimately repeats within one sheet is not mismatched); rows found in both sheets are dropped from history. This removed 21,932 rows (830 invoices) - all of them already outside the history window by date, so the explicit dedup did not change the final counts but is kept as a safeguard and is reported by the script.

### 8.2 Ranking, evaluation and visit scope
- **Out-of-sample:** pick frequency (order-line count per SKU, as in Module 1) and ABC classes (same 80% / 95% cut-offs, via Module 1's `abc_class`) are computed from the history orders only. SKUs without picks in the history get frequency 0 and class C, and (via `slot_full_velocity` / `slot_class_based`) are placed in the farthest slots. All SKUs of the split's universe still receive a slot.
- **In-sample reference:** the same computation on the future/evaluation orders themselves - not equal to Module 2's full-year results, since it ranks and evaluates on a different (or, for year-over-year, differently windowed) set of orders.
- **Evaluation:** the same layout, S-shape routing and 10 random seeds as Module 2, run on the future orders of each split. Reduction = 1 - distance of the scenario / mean distance of the random seeds evaluated on the same visits.
- **visit_scope = "seen_only_visits":** the same evaluation, restricted to visits of SKUs that had at least one pick in the history period, each against its own 10-seed random baseline computed on that restricted visit set. Orders made up entirely of unseen SKUs (298 of 11,706 for 6+6 months; 673 of 18,957 for year-over-year) have no visits left and drop out of this scope entirely - `n_orders_evaluated` in the output records this.
- **Decomposition:** `new_sku_effect_pp = reduction(seen_only_visits) - reduction(all_visits)`, in percentage points. It is the extra decline caused specifically by having to slot SKUs with no ranking history; the remaining gap between the seen-only reduction and the in-sample reduction is ordinary pick-frequency drift among already-known SKUs.
- **Caveats:** two splits, not a distribution of splits - treat the numbers as indicative, not a confidence interval. The seen-only comparison changes both the numerator (distance) and the order/visit set, so it isolates "what if there were no new SKUs", not a controlled experiment that holds everything else fixed.

## 9. Routing sensitivity (Module 5)
- **Purpose:** Modules 2-4 assume S-shape routing throughout. Module 5 checks whether the ranking of slotting scenarios - and the identity of the best "precision" scenario - is robust to that choice, using two more routing policies and a 4th slotting scenario, on the same layout and the full year of orders (no sampling).

### 9.1 Return routing
- Every aisle containing a pick is entered from the front cross aisle, walked in to its farthest pick, and walked straight back out - never traversed end to end.
- Horizontal component: 2 * x(rightmost aisle with a pick), same as S-shape (the picker still has to reach the farthest aisle and come back). Vertical component: 2 * (farthest pick), summed over every aisle with a pick.
- Verified in `tests/test_routing.py` against two hand-calculated routes (two adjacent aisles, picks at 20 m and at 40 m) that also demonstrate the crossover with S-shape: at 20 m Return is shorter (89 m vs. 109 m); at 40 m S-shape is shorter (109 m vs. 169 m), because S-shape's cost does not depend on pick depth while Return's does.

### 9.2 Largest-gap routing
- Standard heuristic (e.g. Roodbergen & de Koster, 2001): the first and last non-empty aisle (by x) are each traversed end to end, like S-shape. Every aisle strictly between them is entered from **both** the front and the back cross aisle, walking only up to the boundary of its single largest unpicked gap - the gap can also be the stretch between the front cross aisle and the nearest pick, or between the farthest pick and the back cross aisle - and that largest gap is never walked.
- A middle aisle's vertical contribution is 2 * (aisle length - largest gap). If there is only one non-empty aisle in the order, it is entered and left from the front only (2 * farthest pick) - the same single-aisle case as S-shape and Return. Horizontal component: 2 * x(rightmost aisle), unchanged.
- Verified against two hand-calculated 3-aisle routes (an internal gap between two picks, and a gap at the front boundary of the middle aisle: 155 m and 125 m) and against largest-gap reducing to the same value as S-shape when there is no middle aisle to skip a gap in (2 aisles: 115 m both ways).

### 9.3 Aisle-based velocity (4th slotting scenario)
- SKUs are ranked by pick frequency, as in Full velocity. Unlike Full velocity, which ranks ALL slots of the warehouse by straight-line walking distance, this ranks slots aisle by aisle: the fastest SKUs fill aisle 0 completely (both racks, 100 slots, nearest positions first) before any SKU is placed in aisle 1, and so on. Deterministic, one run.
- Motivation: a simpler rule than a full distance ranking - "fill the nearest aisle, then the next" - that a warehouse could implement without computing exact walking distance for every slot.

### 9.4 Evaluation grid and result
- 4 scenarios (Random, Class-based ABC, Full velocity, Aisle-based velocity) x 3 routing policies (S-shape, Return, Largest gap), on all 19,773 orders of the last 12 months. Random and Class-based ABC use the same 10 seeds as Module 2. Reduction = 1 - distance of the scenario / mean distance of the random seeds **under the same routing policy** - each routing policy has its own random baseline, since absolute distances are not comparable across policies.
- Before writing any output, the script re-reads `data/processed/scenario_results.csv` and asserts that S-shape x {Random, Class-based ABC, Full velocity} matches it exactly (933 / 707 / 635 m).
- **Result: the best-performing scenario is not the same under every routing policy.** Aisle-based velocity is shortest under S-shape (587 m, -37.1%) and Largest gap (493 m, -31.9%); Full velocity is shortest under Return (593 m, -43.6%), where Aisle-based velocity is worse than Full velocity but still beats Class-based ABC (695 m vs. 714 m). Mechanism: S-shape and Largest gap charge a cost per aisle visited that is close to fixed (a full traversal, or up to the largest gap) almost regardless of pick depth, so minimising the *number of aisles touched* - what Aisle-based velocity does, by packing fast SKUs into complete aisles - matters more than minimising raw walking distance. Return's cost is a direct there-and-back distance to each pick, which is exactly what Full velocity's ranking criterion (straight-line distance from the depot) is built to minimise.
- Class-based ABC stays a robust middle choice under all three policies (-20.0% to -32.0% vs. random), without needing to know which routing heuristic the warehouse actually uses.

## 10. Re-slotting policies (Module 6)
- **Purpose:** every earlier module compares one-time layouts. Module 6 adds the recurring cost of keeping a layout up to date - a SKU that changes slot has to be physically moved - and asks which update policy gives the best distance/labour trade-off, and how sensitive that answer is to the assumed cost of a move.
- **Setup:** reuses Module 4's year-over-year split. The layout starts ranked on the prior year (Dec 2009 - Nov 2010, exactly Module 4's history snapshot) and is evaluated month by month over the following year (Dec 2010 - Nov 2011). A policy that updates does so at the start of a month using a **rolling 12-month window** of data ending the day before that month starts (no look-ahead: an update for month *m* never sees month *m*'s own orders). SKUs with pick_lines = 0 in that window are ranked last (class C) by default. A **move** is a SKU whose slot changes at an update; the starting layout is never counted.
- **Shared precomputation:** 12 rolling monthly pick-frequency/ABC-class snapshots and 12 monthly order-visit slices are computed once and reused by every policy; a policy that only updates once (P1, P2, P3, Random, the Oracle) is evaluated over the full year in one vectorised call instead of a 12-month loop. Total runtime was 218 seconds for the full grid (precompute + 135 policy x seed runs), well under the 15-minute budget, so no sampling was used.
- **hybrid_ranks(pick_lines, sku_class, N, rng, new_sku_rule):** the single rule behind P1, P2, P3, and the "fresh" recompute inside P5/P6/P7. The nearest N slots are individually ranked by velocity (ties by SKU index, stable sort, same mechanics as `slot_full_velocity`); the remaining slots are zoned A/B/C exactly as `slot_class_based` (same RNG call pattern), so **N=0 reproduces P1/`slot_class_based` exactly and N=n_skus reproduces P2/`slot_full_velocity` exactly** - both are used as a hard sanity check against Module 4's stored year-over-year numbers. `new_sku_rule="start_of_b"` (used by P7) pulls SKUs with pick_lines = 0 out of C and places them as their own group immediately nearer than the regular B group; the B block grows by that many slots and C shrinks by the same amount.
- **P1-P3 (static):** ranked once, at month 0, on the history-year snapshot; never updated. P3's N is swept over {50, 100, 200, 300, 500}; the N with the lowest avg. distance per order (found to be 500, the top of the grid - not necessarily the global optimum) is reused by P5, P6 and P7's hybrid variant.
- **P4 (periodic full velocity):** `hybrid_ranks(..., N=n_skus)` fully recomputed at the start of every month or every quarter (quarters start at evaluation months 0, 3, 6, 9). Every SKU whose position differs from the previous layout counts as a move.
- **P5/P6 (incremental/throttled):** a persistent per-SKU state (current slot, and the top-N/class label recorded at its last placement) carries across periods. At each update, a **trigger rule** decides which SKUs move:
  - P5: a SKU moves if its top-N membership or ABC class differs from what was recorded at its last placement.
  - P6: a SKU moves if (a) its class changed since its last placement, or (b) it stayed in the top N but its overall velocity rank has drifted by more than T positions since its last placement (T in {25, 50, 100}); a SKU entering or leaving the top N always moves.
  - Movers are re-assigned by computing a **fresh, from-scratch `hybrid_ranks`** for that period (used only to order the movers relative to each other - nearest-desired first) and reusing exactly the set of slots the movers themselves vacate (nearest vacated slot to the nearest-desired mover). Non-movers keep their exact previous slot. This is a deliberate simplification: physical zone boundaries are **not** redrawn to their "ideal" size every period (which would force moving non-movers too, defeating the point of a throttled policy); they are left to drift with real churn, which is the realistic cost of a low-move policy.
- **P7 (new-SKU rule):** re-runs P1 and whichever of P1-P6 had the lowest avg. distance per order (P4 monthly, in this run) with `new_sku_rule="start_of_b"`. Applying it to a policy with no B/C zone at all (pure full velocity, N=n_skus) is a no-op by construction - confirmed identical to the plain policy in the output.
- **Reference rows, not policies:** Random (10 seeds, never updated) and an Oracle full-velocity layout ranked on the evaluation year's *own* frequency (look-ahead; an upper bound, excluded from "best policy" comparisons).
- **Metrics:** avg. distance per order and annual walking hours (distance / 1.0 m/s) exactly as in Modules 2-5; annual moves summed over every update after month 0; total annual hours = walking hours + annual moves x minutes-per-move / 60, computed separately for 2, 5 and 10 minutes/move.
- **Result:** P6 (N=500, T=100) and P5 (N=500, monthly) are in a practical tie for the lowest total cost at 2 min/move (3,910 vs. 3,911 h/year); P2 (static full velocity, never updated) overtakes both between about 4.0 and 4.4 min/move (the exact break-even against P6 and P5 respectively) and stays cheapest at 5 and 10 min/move. P1 (plain static class-based ABC) is dominated at every move-time tested. P4 monthly reshuffles on the order of 96% of the catalogue every month (40,161 moves/year) purely from small frequency ties shifting in the long tail, and is never worth it in the tested cost range.

## 11. Optimal routing (Module 7)
- **Purpose:** Modules 2-6 use simple routing rules. Module 7 measures how far each rule is from the shortest possible route for every order, and whether the best slotting scenario changes when routing is optimal. Script: `src/07_optimal_routing.py`; it imports the layout, orders, slotting functions and the S-shape / Return / Largest-gap functions from Modules 2 and 5 and changes none of them.

### 11.1 Graph model
- Same layout as section 4 (40 aisles, 50 m long, 3 m pitch, depot at the left end of the front cross aisle, x = 0, y = 0).
- **Nodes:** the depot, every distinct pick location (aisle, y) of the order, and the front (y = 0) and back (y = 50 m) end of every aisle. Two SKUs facing each other across an aisle share one location.
- **Edges:** along each aisle between consecutive nodes (front end, picks sorted by y, back end); along the front and back cross aisles between neighbouring aisle ends (3 m); depot to the front end of aisle 0 (1.5 m). Cross-aisle width and lateral movement inside an aisle are ignored, as in section 4.
- **Shortest paths (closed form):** same aisle |y1 - y2|; different aisles |x1 - x2| + min(y1 + y2, 2L - y1 - y2), i.e. via the front or the back cross aisle (a detour through a third aisle is never shorter); depot to (x, y): x + y. `graph_distance_matrix` builds the explicit graph and runs Dijkstra (`scipy.sparse.csgraph`); the tests check that both give identical matrices on a hand-built 3-aisle example (including two picks at one location) and on 200 random orders across all 40 aisles.

### 11.2 TSP set-up (OR-Tools)
- One TSP per order: start at the depot, visit every pick location, return to the depot. Solved with the Google OR-Tools routing solver (`ortools` 9.9, one vehicle, depot = node 0). The distance matrix is passed as integers in half-metres (all coordinates are multiples of 0.5 m, so this is exact).
- **Warm start:** the search starts from the better of the Combined and Largest-gap visiting sequences (the order in which each rule first reaches each location). Walking a rule's visiting order along shortest paths can never be longer than the rule itself (tested on 300 random orders), so the OR-Tools result can never be longer than Largest gap or Combined, and therefore never longer than S-shape or Return either (section 11.3).
- **Search:** orders with at most 40 distinct locations (84.8% of order x layout cases) use guided local search, stopped after 50 solutions - a deterministic stopping rule, unlike a time limit, so results do not depend on machine load (a 1 s wall-clock cap is kept as a safety net). Larger orders use the default OR-Tools local search (2-opt, or-opt, relocate, exchange, Lin-Kernighan, ...) until no move improves the tour.
- **Why guided local search:** a first full run used plain local search for every order and missed the exact optimum in 23 of 800 validation cases with at most 10 locations (worst: +9.8%). One case is kept as a test: a 5-location order where plain local search stops at 402 m, a true local optimum under swap, 2-opt and relocate, while the optimum is 386 m and needs two consecutive moves. Guided local search escapes it. On larger orders, the cheaper alternatives tested (TSP-opt, LNS operators, a GLS time limit proportional to order size) either did not close the gap or were far too slow.
- **Time budget:** a 500-order timing run (8 worker processes, i7-3770) estimated 53 min for all orders with 10 seeds for Random and Class-based ABC - over the 30-minute budget, and still ~37 min with a time cap on large orders. The per-layout time measured in the first full run (~95 s) confirmed that 10 seeds (22 layouts) would take ~35 min. The run therefore uses **3 seeds (0-2)** for Random and Class-based ABC; Full velocity and Aisle-based velocity are deterministic. The GLS threshold of 40 locations was chosen to stay inside the budget (for 80 locations the timing run estimated 38 min; scaled by the ratio of the first full run's actual to estimated time, 12.7 / 19.1, that is ~26 min plus validation - too close to the limit). Final run: 23.8 min (23.0 min routing + 0.7 min validation). No order sampling.

### 11.3 Combined routing (definition used)
- Aisles with picks are visited left to right. For each one the picker chooses between (a) traversing it end to end, cost L, which switches them to the other cross aisle, and (b) entering from the cross aisle they are on and walking back out the same way: from the front 2 x (farthest pick), from the back 2 x (L - nearest pick). The route must end at the front cross aisle.
- The choice is made by **dynamic programming** over the picker's side: with f / b the shortest vertical distance so far ending at the front / back, each aisle updates `f' = min(f + 2*y_max, b + L)` and `b' = min(b + 2*(L - y_min), f + L)`, starting from f = 0, b = infinity; the answer is f after the last aisle. Horizontal distance is 2 x x(rightmost aisle), as for the other rules. This is the "combined" heuristic of Roodbergen & de Koster (2001).
- **Combined <= min(S-shape, Return) on every order:** S-shape (traverse every aisle, return-trip the last one when the count is odd) and Return (return-trip every aisle from the front) are both feasible choice sequences inside the DP, so the DP optimum can never be longer than either. Asserted on all 158,184 order x layout cases and on 500 random orders in the tests. A *greedy* per-aisle version ("pick whichever is shorter for this aisle alone") would **not** have this property: with two aisles, a single pick at 26 m in the first and at 1 m in the second, greedy traverses the first aisle (50 < 52) and must then traverse the second to get back to the front (vertical 100 m), while Return needs 52 + 2 = 54 m. This is why the DP definition is used.
- Combined is **not** always shorter than Largest gap, which uses a move Combined does not have (entering a middle aisle from both ends).
- Verified against a hand-calculated 4-aisle route (picks at 45, 48, 3, 2 m: Combined 129 m vs. S-shape 221 m and Return 217 m), and vectorised vs. scalar on 300 random orders.

### 11.4 Validation
- **Exact check (orders with <= 10 SKUs):** 200 random orders with at most 10 distinct SKUs (seed 7), each routed in all 4 scenarios (seed 0 for Random and Class-based ABC) = 800 cases with 1-10 locations (580 of them with 3 or more). The OR-Tools result is compared with the exact optimum from Held-Karp dynamic programming (itself tested against brute force on 60 random instances). **Result: identical in 800 of 800 cases; mean and maximum difference 0.0000%.**
- **Larger orders (diagnostic, not exact):** 100 random orders with more than 10 SKUs (11-158 locations, Random seed 0), each also solved with a 3-second guided local search from the same warm start. OR-Tools as used in the main run was identical in 74 of 100; mean gap +0.23%, median 0.00%, maximum +2.41%. A 3 s GLS does not prove optimality either, so this measures the residual gap against a stronger search, not against the true optimum. Orders above 40 locations (plain local search) are the part of "Optimal" with the largest uncertainty.
- **Consistency:** Full velocity and Aisle-based velocity under S-shape / Return / Largest gap reproduce Module 5's `routing_sensitivity.csv` exactly (asserted by the script). On every order x layout case, Optimal <= each of the four rules (asserted by the script and in `tests/test_optimal_routing.py`).
- **Hand-calculated example (tests):** aisles 1 and 2, one pick at 49 m in aisle 1 and one at 5 m in aisle 2. Optimal = 1.5 + 49 + 1 + 3 + 45 + 5 + 4.5 = 109 m (up aisle 1, across the back, down aisle 2), matched by OR-Tools, Held-Karp, S-shape, Largest gap and Combined; Return = 117 m.

### 11.5 Metrics and result
- **Avg. distance per order:** mean over orders, then over seeds, as in Modules 2-5. **Excess vs. optimal:** per order, rule / optimal - 1, pooled over all orders (and seeds); reported as mean and median. `excess_of_avg_distance_pct` in the CSV is the same ratio on the average distances. **Change vs. random:** vs. the random baseline of the same routing policy.
- **Result:** the best simple rule per scenario is 4.4-8.0% above optimal on average (Largest gap for Random and Aisle-based velocity, Combined for Class-based ABC and Full velocity); S-shape is 29-42% and Return 21-45% above it. With optimal routing, Aisle-based velocity has the shortest average (454 m), then Full velocity (462 m), Class-based ABC (531 m) and Random (684 m) - the same winner as under S-shape and Largest gap; Return and Combined favour Full velocity.

## 12. Multi-picker congestion simulation (Module 8a)
- **Purpose:** Modules 2-7 route one picker alone. Module 8a simulates a team working at the same time, with aisles that hold a limited number of pickers, to measure time lost to congestion, its effect on the slotting and routing conclusions, and the team size needed. Script: `src/08_congestion_simulation.py` (SimPy 4.1, discrete-event). It imports the layout, slotting and S-shape functions from Modules 2 and 5 and the optimal-route solver from Module 7, and changes none of them.

### 12.1 Workload check (before the simulation)
- Source: the 19,773 orders of the last 12 months (1 Dec 2010 - 9 Dec 2011), 305 working days (no Saturdays in the data). Order time = timestamp of the invoice's first line.
- Orders per day: mean 64.8, median 62, busiest day 141 (2 Dec 2010), quietest 11. Busiest hour: 32 orders (10 May 2011, 15:00-16:00).
- Time per order = walking (route length / 1 m/s) + 10 s x distinct SKUs + 60 s; mean 26.2 SKUs per order. Mean time per order and daily workload (mean / median / busiest day):
  - Random, S-shape: 1,258 s; 22.6 / 21.5 / 50.6 h per day
  - Random, optimal: 1,006 s; 18.1 / 16.9 / 42.1 h
  - Class-based ABC, S-shape: 1,031 s; 18.6 / 17.2 / 43.2 h
  - Class-based ABC, optimal: 855 s; 15.4 / 14.2 / 36.9 h
  - Full velocity, S-shape: 956 s; 17.2 / 15.9 / 41.0 h
  - Full velocity, optimal: 784 s; 14.1 / 13.0 / 35.0 h
  - The busiest day is 5 Dec 2011 in every case; the busiest hour carries 9-16 h of work.
- **Shift hours from the data:** 98.1% of orders arrive between 08:00 and 18:00 (first order of the day: median 08:36; last: median 17:23; extremes 06:20 and 20:18). The shift is therefore 08:00-18:00 (10 h). Orders that arrive before 08:00 wait for the shift; orders that arrive after 18:00 are picked in overtime.
- **Rough team size at x1:** mean daily workload / 10 h = 2-3 pickers on an average day, 4-6 on the busiest day. Scaled by volume: about 8-12 pickers at x5 and 15-23 at x10.
- **Picker ranges (5 counts per volume, chosen for the runtime budget):** x1: 2, 3, 4, 6, 8; x5: 8, 10, 12, 16, 24; x10: 15, 18, 23, 30, 40. The lower end is the smallest team for which the best layout/route keeps up on an average day (x1: 14.1 h / 10 h -> 2; x5: 70 h -> 8; x10: 141 h -> 15). The upper end is about 1.7-2.7x the average-day requirement of the worst layout/route (Random, S-shape: 3 / 12 / 23 pickers), meant to be far enough to see where extra pickers stop helping. A finer grid (7-9 counts per volume) and sensitivities at every point were estimated at ~100 min, over the 30-minute budget; this 5-point grid with a reduced sensitivity set (section 12.4) took 30.6 min.

### 12.2 Simulation model
- **One independent simulation per calendar day** (the queue is empty at the start of every day because all leftover work is finished the same day).
- **Arrivals:** each order enters a FIFO queue at its order time. **Pull:** an idle picker takes the next order. Pickers are present from 08:00.
- **Picking an order:** 60 s set-up/hand-over (placed at the start), then the route: for every aisle visit the picker walks along the cross aisle to the aisle end (no capacity limit), requests a place in the aisle, waits if the aisle is full, walks inside it while picking (10 s per SKU), leaves at an end and releases the place; finally walks back to the depot. Speed 1 m/s.
- **Aisles:** 40 capacity-limited resources (default capacity 1). The place is held from entering the aisle until leaving it at either end, also while walking to and from the picks. Waiting is the only blocking policy (no passing, no rerouting, no re-sequencing). Requests are served first-come-first-served. Because a picker releases an aisle before requesting the next one, the model cannot deadlock. Cross aisles and the depot have no capacity limit.
- **Routes:**
  - *S-shape:* Module 2's route, walked aisle by aisle (each aisle with a pick traversed front -> back or back -> front alternately; with an odd number of aisles the last one is entered from the front and left from its farthest pick). Walking length equals `s_shape_distance` exactly.
  - *Optimal:* the visiting sequence from Module 7's solver (same warm start and search settings), cached in `data/processed/optimal_tours.pkl` (not committed). Between two locations in the same aisle the picker stays inside; otherwise they leave by the end that gives the shorter path (front on ties), cross, and enter the next aisle from the same side. A location can therefore be reached in several separate visits to the same aisle. Walking length equals the tour length. The cached tours reproduce Module 7's stored optimal distances on 19,773 of 19,773 orders in every layout.
- **Layouts:** Random and Class-based ABC with seed 0 only: Module 2's 10 seeds differ by +-0.3% (Random, 930-936 m) and +-0.6% (ABC, 703-711 m) in S-shape distance per order, small next to the effects measured here, and every extra seed would add a full set of simulations. Full velocity and Aisle-based velocity are deterministic.
- **End of day / overtime:** work in the queue at 18:00 is finished. Overtime per day = sum over pickers of (time the picker completes their last order of the day - 18:00), if positive, in picker-hours. This is 8a's original definition, kept in its CSV; the overtime in the README's 8a table was corrected in Module 8c (section 14.2). Overtime counts only work done after 18:00, not idle picker time.

### 12.3 Volume replication (x5, x10)
- Each real order is copied k times (k = 5 or 10). Copy 0 keeps its real time; copies 1..k-1 get a uniform random time shift in [-30, +30] min (fixed seed 8), clipped so that every copy stays on the same calendar date (00:00-23:59:59). The SKU set, and therefore the route, of every copy is the same as the original's. The daily and hourly profile and the order-size mix are preserved; the shift spreads copies so that they do not all arrive at the same second.

### 12.4 Metrics, optimal team size and sensitivity
- **Aisle wait per order** = total time spent waiting at aisle ends. **Waiting / work time** = total aisle wait / total work time (work time = set-up + walking + picking + aisle wait). **Queue wait** = order arrival -> a picker starts it. **Cycle time** = order arrival -> order completed. **Utilisation** = total work time / paid time (pickers x 10 h x days + overtime); waiting in an aisle counts as work. **Orders per shift hour** = completions per clock hour between 08:00 and 18:00, averaged over all (day, hour) pairs. **Front-aisle share** = share of all aisle waiting that happens in the 5 aisles nearest the depot.
- **Gain vs. Random** = Random's mean work time per order minus the layout's, at the same volume, team size and route; "without congestion" uses the work time minus aisle wait (identical to a run with unlimited aisle capacity), "with congestion" the simulated work time.
- **Optimal team size:** the smallest team size in the grid after which moving to the next grid point has a cycle-time elasticity, (dC/C) / (dn/n), above -0.25 - a 10% larger team cuts mean cycle time by less than 2.5%. If no step meets this, the result is reported as ">= largest team tested". If extra pickers stop helping because the busiest aisle is saturated, the "optimal" team is small but cycle times stay long; these cases are flagged as bottleneck-limited.
- **Bottleneck check:** for each layout and route, the total in-aisle time demanded per day of the busiest aisle was computed directly from the routes. Busiest aisle (S-shape) at x10: Aisle-based velocity 16.1 h, Full velocity 12.4 h, Class-based ABC 8.6 h, Random 5.4 h per 10-hour shift (optimal routes: 15.3 / 10.6 / 7.3 / 4.2 h). Above 10 h, no team size can clear that aisle within the shift with capacity 1.
- **Sensitivity (reduced for runtime):** x10 volume, 23 pickers (fixed before any results were seen: the middle of the x10 grid, about what Random/S-shape needs on an average day), Random and Full velocity, both routes: (a) aisle capacity 2, (b) 15 s per SKU.

### 12.5 Tests (`tests/test_congestion.py`)
- One picker, unlimited aisles: no waiting, and every order's time = 60 s + picks + route length / 1 m/s, on synthetic orders and on a real day; route lengths equal Module 2's S-shape and Module 7's optimal distances (200 random orders; all real orders of all four layouts).
- One picker with capacity 1 never waits; very large capacity gives zero waiting on a busy synthetic day (and capacity 1 on the same day does give waiting).
- Hand-calculated blocking: two pickers, two identical one-aisle orders at 08:00, capacity 1 -> the second waits exactly the first picker's 51 s in the aisle; with capacity 2 nobody waits. Shift handling: an order at 06:00 starts at 08:00; one at 17:59:50 produces the expected overtime.
- Reproducibility: two runs with the same inputs and seed give identical results; replication keeps every copy on its original date.

## 13. Refined congestion model (Module 8b)
- **Purpose:** Module 8a (section 12) is kept unchanged as the *strict model*, in which a whole aisle holds one picker. Module 8b refines the aisle model, tests a traffic-spreading layout and a skip-and-return policy, and adds a cost-based team size. Script: `src/08b_congestion_refined.py`; it imports Module 8a (orders, shift 08:00-18:00, x5/x10 replication, pick and handling times, tour cache) and the Module 2/5/7 functions, and changes none of them.

### 13.1 Segment model
- Each 50 m aisle is split into **10 segments of 5 m** (segment k covers y in [5k, 5k + 5)). Each segment is a SimPy resource with capacity 1 (**narrow aisle**) or 2 (**wide aisle**). Cross aisles and the depot have no capacity limit, as in 8a.
- A picker's walk through an aisle is pre-computed as a sequence of (segment, seconds) pieces: walking at 1 m/s, split at the 5 m boundaries, plus 10 s per SKU added to the segment where it is picked. While a piece lasts the picker holds that segment; moving to the next segment means requesting it.
- The walk itself (entry end, order of the picks, exit end) is exactly the Module 2 S-shape route or the Module 7 optimal tour (same rules as `s_shape_route` / `tour_route` in Module 8a); walking lengths equal Module 2's S-shape and Module 7's optimal distances (tested on 200 random orders and on every real order of two layouts).

### 13.2 Deadlock and how it is avoided ("step aside")
- With hold-and-wait (keep the current segment until the next one is granted), two pickers walking towards each other in neighbouring narrow segments would wait for each other forever (A in segment 3 waits for 4, B in 4 waits for 3), and longer cycles are possible.
- **Rule chosen:** a picker releases their current segment *before* requesting the next one. Nobody ever holds a segment while waiting for another, so the "hold and wait" condition for deadlock never holds and no circular wait can form. Physically: a blocked picker steps aside (to the rack face or back to the segment boundary) and lets the occupant pass. The picker therefore only waits when the 5 m stretch they want to enter is occupied - "waiting only when reaching the same spot".
- **Why this rule:** one-way aisles do not fit the routes used here (S-shape traverses aisles in alternating directions, and the last aisle of an S-shape route and many optimal-route visits are entered and left from the same end), and an explicit retreat-and-retry protocol would need extra rules for who backs off. Stepping aside is the simplest rule that is deadlock-free by construction. It is slightly optimistic: a waiting picker does not block others, and two pickers can always pass each other once one of them yields.
- Verified: on a crowded synthetic day (40 pickers, 600 orders in 10 minutes, narrow aisles, both policies) the simulation ends with every order completed; a hand-calculated two-picker example checks waiting at the same segment (entry: 5 s; at the turn of a return trip: 6 s) and no waiting in different segments.

### 13.3 Spread velocity layout
- SKUs ranked by pick frequency (as in Full velocity). The first K aisles (K = 5, 10, 20) are filled round-robin, front first: slot order = position (depth), then rack side, then aisle - rank 1 goes to aisle 1 position 1 left rack, rank 2 to aisle 2 position 1 left, ..., rank K+1 to aisle 1 position 1 right rack, and so on until the first K aisles are full (K x 100 slots); remaining SKUs continue aisle by aisle as in Aisle-based velocity. K = 1 is exactly Module 5's Aisle-based velocity (tested).

### 13.4 Skip-and-return policy
- Just before walking to the next aisle on the route (after the order set-up for the first aisle, after leaving an aisle for the others), the picker looks at that aisle's entry segment on the side they are on. If it is full or has a queue, and another aisle follows on the route, the two aisle visits swap places: the picker does the following aisle first and comes back to the skipped one straight after. Each aisle can be skipped at most once, and the replacement aisle is taken regardless of its own state (no chains of skips). The decision uses the state at decision time; it can change during the walk.
- Swapping two consecutive aisle visits never changes which cross aisle (front/back) the picker ends on - each visit either switches sides (traverse) or not (enter and leave by the same end), and the order of two such steps does not change the result - so the rest of the route stays valid and still ends at the front. A visit entered from the other end than planned is walked in mirror order (picks sorted from the actual entry end; a traverse still leaves by the opposite end, a return trip by the same end). Cross-aisle walking is recomputed from the actual positions; extra walking is counted.
- How a WMS would know a segment is occupied is not modelled; the rule is applied with perfect information at the decision moment.

### 13.5 Cost-based team size and overtime definition
- **Service level:** (a) >= 95% of orders completed on the day they arrive (before midnight), and (b) on >= 95% of days, overtime per picker <= 30 min. "Per picker" is the day's total overtime / team size: a literal per-person limit cannot be met on 18% of days (55 of 305), which have an order arriving after 18:30 (almost all Thursdays).
- **Overtime definition (changed from 8a):** 8a counted overtime from 18:00 until each picker's last completion. On Thursday evenings the pull queue hands each late order to a different idle picker, so idle waiting was counted as overtime (x1, 8 pickers, Full velocity: Thursdays 5.9 pickers "in overtime", 7.4 picker-hours, vs. 1.95 hours of actual work after 18:00; 15% of days over the limit vs. 2% on actual work). 8b's service level and cost use **overtime work**: time spent on orders after 18:00, summed over pickers. The 8a definition is kept in `overtime_picker_h_per_day` for comparison. This was found after the first 8b run; only the team-size set (30 configurations) was re-run with the new metric, and all its queueing metrics reproduced the first run exactly (asserted, max difference 2.8e-14). For the reference and spread/skip rows, the service-level and cost columns are left empty.
- **Unit cost** = pickers x 10 h x days x wage + overtime work hours x 1.5 x wage, wage = 1 unit/hour (parameter `WAGE_PER_H`); reported per day and per order.
- **Cost-based team size** = the smallest team in the grid that meets the service level; **speed-based** = Module 8a's elasticity rule; the cheapest team in the grid is reported as well.

### 13.6 Scope and runtime
- The full factorial (7 layouts x 2 routes x 2 aisle models x 2 policies x 3 volumes x 5 team sizes = 840 configurations) was estimated at ~10 h (single-process timings: x1 ~34 s, x5 ~187 s, x10 ~360 s per configuration, about 4-5 times Module 8a because every aisle visit is now ~10 segment events instead of one). The user chose a core scope, S-shape only: (1) x10 / 23 pickers, Random and Full velocity, narrow and wide, wait; (2) x10 / 23 pickers, narrow, all 7 layouts x {wait, skip}; (3) Class-based ABC and Full velocity, narrow, wait, every volume x team sizes x1: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8; x5: 10, 12, 14, 16, 20; x10: 18, 23, 28, 34, 40. 44 distinct configurations, 45.8 min (estimate ~40 min) plus a 23.6 min re-run of set (3) for the overtime fix.
- **Team-size ranges:** from Module 8a, the speed-based knee was 6 at x1 and beyond 24 / 40 at x5 / x10 in the strict model; the lower ends are the smallest teams with an acceptable queue in 8a, the upper ends the largest the runtime allowed. They turned out too narrow to reach the cost-based service level at x5 and x10.
- **Bottleneck check:** for each layout, the daily in-aisle time demanded of the busiest aisle and the busiest segment was computed from the routes. At x10 (S-shape): Aisle-based velocity 16.1 h per busiest aisle but 2.2 h per busiest segment; Spread K = 5 / 10 / 20: 11.4 / 9.3 / 7.1 h per aisle, 1.8 / 1.6 / 1.3 h per segment; Full velocity 12.4 h / 2.2 h (shift: 10 h).

## 14. Order cut-off and staffing (Module 8c)
- **Purpose:** Module 8b's service level could not be met at x5 and x10, mainly because orders keep arriving on Thursday evenings until about 20:00. Module 8c replaces "every order the same day" with an order cut-off, and corrects Module 8a's overtime metric. Script: `src/08c_cutoff_service.py` (imports Module 8b; Module 8b's `simulate_day` got two optional parameters, `deferrable` and `defer_at_s`, whose default keeps the original behaviour - Module 8b's tests still pass and the 24:00 check below reproduces its stored results exactly).

### 14.1 Cut-off model
- An order is **post-cut-off** if it arrives at or after the cut-off (16:00, 17:00 or 18:00) on its day. The queue becomes a priority queue: carried-over and pre-cut-off orders first (FIFO among them), post-cut-off orders only when nothing else is waiting. At 18:00 every post-cut-off order not yet started is withdrawn from the queue; post-cut-off orders arriving after 18:00 never enter it. Withdrawn orders **carry over** to the next working day (the next date with orders in the data), where they enter the queue at midnight and are therefore picked first from 08:00. There is no night picking; pre-cut-off orders still in the queue at 18:00 are finished in overtime, as before.
- Because work carries over, the days are no longer independent: each configuration simulates the 305 working days in order. Orders still unpicked after the last day are counted (none were).
- **Service level:** (a) >= 95% of pre-cut-off orders completed on their arrival day (before midnight); (b) >= 95% of post-cut-off orders completed the same day or by 10:00 (shift start + 2 h) on the next working day; (c) overtime work per picker <= 30 min on >= 95% of days (Module 8b's definition, section 13.5). The brief gives no percentage for (b); the same 95% is used.
- **Unit cost** as in 8b (pickers x 10 h x days + 1.5 x overtime work hours, wage 1 unit/h), per order. Cycle time of a carried order counts the working days between arrival and picking as 24 h each (weekend and holiday gaps are not added).
- **Scope** (the full brief - 3 layouts x 2 policies x 3 cut-offs x 3 volumes x 5 team sizes, 270 configurations - was estimated at ~3 h; the user chose the minimum scope): Full velocity, narrow aisles, S-shape, wait; team sizes x1 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 (8b's grid); x5 14, 18, 24 and x10 28, 36, 44 (shifted above 8b's grids, where 8b never met its service level). 33 configurations plus the 24:00 check; 27.5 min including the Module 8a re-simulations.
- **Checks:** with a 24:00 cut-off nothing is deferred and the model reproduces Module 8b's x1 / 4 pickers / Full velocity row on 7 metrics with zero difference (asserted). Tests: the priority-queue path without deferrals equals the plain FIFO path; a 24:00 year equals independent days; a hand-calculated carry-over example (an order after the cut-off but within the shift is picked the same day; one after 18:00 is first in the next morning's queue); pre-cut-off orders are served before post-cut-off ones; two runs with the same inputs are identical.

### 14.2 Module 8a overtime, corrected
- Module 8a's overtime (from 18:00 until each picker's last order) also counted idle evening waiting (section 13.5). The overtime **work** (time spent on orders after 18:00) needs per-order start and end times, which 8a did not store, so the 24 configurations in the 8a README table (x1 / 4, x5 / 12, x10 / 23 pickers; 4 layouts; S-shape and optimal routes) were re-simulated with Module 8a's own code. The re-simulation reproduced the stored aisle wait and old overtime exactly (max difference 5.7e-14, asserted). Old and corrected values: `data/processed/congestion_simulation_overtime_work.csv`; the README table shows both, the old one marked as the wrong definition.
- The correction matters at x1 (for example Full velocity / optimal: 1.23 -> 0.51 picker-hours per day; Random / S-shape: 2.11 -> 1.59), where evening orders are a large share of the late work, and is small at x5 and x10 (at most 1.4 picker-hours per day), where most overtime is real work. The other 104 8a configurations in its CSV keep the old definition.

## 15. Known limitations
- The layout is synthetic; absolute distances depend on its dimensions, relative differences between scenarios on the layout shape and order size.
- The main results (Modules 2-3) are in-sample, which favours the velocity-based scenarios; the out-of-sample check (section 8) shows a realistic saving is roughly half of the in-sample figures, and that most of the gap is attributable to SKUs with no pick history rather than to the ranking method itself.
- Zones for class-based slotting follow walking distance from the depot, not aisle boundaries; an aisle-based ranking was tested only as a 4th, separate scenario (section 9.3), not as an alternative zoning for class-based ABC. Co-occurrence-based slotting was not tested.
- Module 6 models the recurring cost of re-slotting as moves x a flat minutes-per-move, evaluated only under S-shape routing; it does not model truck/labour scheduling, batching of moves, SKU handling difficulty, or the lag between deciding to re-slot and completing the move. P3/P5/P6's N grid stopped at 500 (the task's specified grid) while performance was still improving, so the true optimum N may be higher.
- Module 7's "Optimal" is exact on every validated order with <= 10 SKUs, but orders above 40 locations use plain local search and are only checked, on a sample, against a longer (also heuristic) search; its Random and Class-based ABC figures use 3 seeds instead of 10 because of the runtime budget.
- Module 8c covers one layout and policy (Full velocity, wait) and a coarse team grid at x5 / x10 (the smallest compliant x5 team is only bracketed between 18 and 24; at x10 it lies above 44). Carried orders are assumed to be picked from 08:00 in arrival order; customer-specific promises and truck departure times are not modelled.
- Module 8b's step-aside rule lets a blocked picker yield their segment, which slightly understates blocking in narrow aisles; it covers S-shape routing only (core scope), uses perfect information for skip decisions, and its team-size grid did not reach the cost-based service level at x5 and x10. Module 8a's overtime counts idle evening time (see section 13.5; corrected for the README table in section 14.2).
- Module 8a blocks whole aisles (one picker per aisle, wherever they are in it) and only lets pickers wait; real pickers can often pass or re-sequence, so capacity 1 is a pessimistic narrow-aisle case and capacity 2 an optimistic one - the results depend strongly on it. Volume is scaled by copying real orders, so the SKU mix does not change with volume. Its picker grid has 5 points per volume, so the optimal team size is only bracketed (">= largest tested") where the knee lies beyond the grid.
- No batching, zone picking or item size/rack capacity constraints elsewhere in the model; congestion is modelled only in Module 8a.
